One project. Five ways to understand it.

Move through the tabs as a research narrative: first the reason, then the system, the sequence, the evidence, and finally the tools.

Why build it?

A laboratory for honest machine learning.

Markets make every modeling weakness visible. A convincing result requires good data, careful statistics, strict chronology, realistic costs, and explanations that survive scrutiny—not merely a high score.

Suppose the market falls six percent. Apple falls three percent, Microsoft falls two percent, and a weak competitor falls eleven percent. An absolute forecast saying “Apple will rise” failed. A relative forecast ranking Microsoft and Apple above the competitor can still be useful.

The objective is not to predict the future perfectly. It is to test whether measurable differences between stocks contain a small, repeatable ranking signal.

That change in question—from direction to ranking—creates a richer research problem. Every date becomes a new comparison across many companies. Momentum asks which stocks have persisted. Reversal asks which have moved too far too quickly. Volatility describes uncertainty. Volume indicates whether a movement attracted unusual participation.

The project joins probability, linear algebra, optimization, algorithms, software design, and visual communication. You learn why a model appears predictive, when the relationship breaks, how costs reduce the result, and how easily future information can accidentally leak into the past.

The final result is not a promise of profit. It is a reproducible scientific argument: here is the hypothesis, here is the data available at the time, here is the test, here are the failures, and here is the uncertainty around the conclusion.

What should it do?

From raw prices to defensible evidence.

The application should separate each responsibility so that data errors, modeling choices, portfolio rules, and visual conclusions can be tested independently.

01 / Data

Acquire and validate

  • Download daily OHLCV observations for one or many tickers.
  • Normalize symbols, dates, columns, and adjusted prices.
  • Cache raw responses so experiments can be reproduced offline.
  • Detect missing rows, duplicate observations, and impossible prices.
  • Report unavailable tickers instead of silently discarding them.
  • Record download time, provider, interval, and universe definition.
02 / Factors

Describe market behavior

  • Calculate short-, medium-, and long-horizon momentum.
  • Represent short-term reversal and distance from moving averages.
  • Measure rolling volatility, range, downside risk, and beta.
  • Detect unusual volume and calculate rolling dollar liquidity.
  • Clip extreme values and normalize factors within each date.
  • Audit feature availability to prevent future information leakage.
03 / Learning

Train without time travel

  • Build one-, five-, and twenty-day forward-return targets.
  • Convert future returns into daily cross-sectional ranks.
  • Use expanding or rolling walk-forward validation windows.
  • Compare equal-weight factors, Ridge, Lasso, and nonlinear models.
  • Save coefficients, parameters, predictions, and split definitions.
  • Reserve a final period that is never used for model selection.
04 / Portfolio

Turn scores into positions

  • Rank all eligible stocks by their predicted relative return.
  • Long the top group and short the bottom group.
  • Apply position limits, liquidity filters, and rebalance schedules.
  • Track gross, net, sector, and market-beta exposure.
  • Calculate turnover before applying transaction-cost assumptions.
  • Separate long-side, short-side, and cost contributions.
05 / Evaluation

Challenge the result

  • Measure daily and rolling Spearman Information Coefficient.
  • Compare return curves for quintiles and deciles.
  • Calculate Sharpe ratio, drawdown, volatility, and hit rate.
  • Stress-test transaction costs and rebalance frequency.
  • Break results down by year, sector, and volatility regime.
  • Compare every complex model against a simple baseline.
06 / Communication

Make research inspectable

  • Display data coverage and missing-value summaries.
  • Plot distributions, correlations, and factor-return relationships.
  • Show prediction ranks, equity, drawdown, turnover, and costs.
  • Explain model coefficients and feature importance.
  • Export experiment configuration, predictions, and metrics.
  • State limitations alongside results—not in fine print.

How does data move?

A one-way path from observation to conclusion.

Information must move forward through time. Features belong to the known past; targets and realized returns belong to the unknown future. Mixing them invalidates the experiment.

01

Experiment configuration

Define symbols, date range, prediction horizon, rebalance frequency, costs, and random seed before viewing results.

02

Yahoo Finance ingestion Implemented

Download OHLCV data and convert it into a consistent date–symbol table with explicit errors.

03

Quality control

Inspect missing observations, duplicates, price relationships, corporate-action adjustments, and history length.

04

Universe selection

Apply point-in-time eligibility, liquidity, price, and minimum-history rules without using future membership.

05

Feature engineering

Calculate momentum, reversal, volatility, volume, beta, and market-relative descriptors using past data only.

06

Cross-sectional preprocessing

Clip outliers and transform every feature into a z-score or percentile relative to stocks available that day.

07

Target generation

Calculate future returns, then rank them within each date to create a robust supervised-learning target.

08

Walk-forward validation

Train on the past, validate on the next period, test later, and use embargo gaps when labels overlap.

09

Model comparison

Start with simple factor averages and Ridge regression before testing trees or boosting.

10

Prediction and ranking

Generate one score per stock per date, then convert scores into stable cross-sectional ranks.

11

Portfolio construction

Long the highest-ranked group, short the lowest, and constrain concentration and unwanted exposure.

12

Backtest and friction

Lag positions correctly, calculate turnover, deduct costs, and attribute performance to long and short books.

13

Evaluation and reporting

Combine predictive metrics, portfolio metrics, robustness tests, visual evidence, and limitations.

What will you use?

A compact stack with clear responsibilities.

Use the smallest tool that makes each research stage explicit. Complexity is earned only when a simpler baseline cannot answer the question.

Language

Python

Coordinates the pipeline, expresses financial calculations clearly, and connects the entire scientific ecosystem.

Market data

yfinance

Supplies accessible historical OHLCV data for a learning project. Suitable for research prototypes, not production execution.

Tables

pandas

Handles date–symbol tables, rolling windows, grouped normalization, joins, missing values, and resampling.

Numerics

NumPy

Provides vectorized arrays and efficient numerical operations for returns, weights, simulation, and metrics.

Learning

scikit-learn

Offers Ridge, Lasso, preprocessing, pipelines, model inspection, and reproducible evaluation primitives.

Exploration

Seaborn

Creates fast statistical views of distributions, correlations, feature behavior, and grouped performance.

Interaction

Streamlit

Turns research outputs into a filterable dashboard without requiring a separate frontend service.

Quality

unittest

Checks date logic, factor calculations, leakage guards, portfolio accounting, and failure behavior.

Reproducibility

Git + uv

Track research changes and lock the Python environment so another machine can reproduce the experiment.

Build an argument, not a prediction.

The measure of success is not a spectacular equity curve. It is whether another researcher can follow the data, reproduce the experiment, identify the assumptions, and understand why the evidence supports—or rejects—the hypothesis.
